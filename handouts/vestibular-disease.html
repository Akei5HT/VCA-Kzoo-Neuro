Balance Problems (Vestibular Disease) in Dogs

The vestibular system — parts of the inner ear and brain — tells your dog which way is up. When it is disrupted, the effects can be frightening to watch, but many dogs recover well.

Signs

  • Head tilt, usually toward the affected side
  • Stumbling, falling or leaning to one side; walking in circles
  • Abnormal eye movements — darting side to side or in circles (nystagmus)
  • Nausea and vomiting (like motion sickness)
  • Reluctance to move; confusion or disorientation

Types

1. Peripheral (inner ear) — usually less serious

  • "Old dog vestibular disease" — common in older dogs, no cause found; usually much better in 2–3 weeks
  • Middle or inner ear infections
  • Head injury
  • Congenital (present from birth)

2. Central (brain) — usually more serious

  • Brain inflammation (infection or immune-mediated)
  • Brain tumors
  • Stroke

Diagnosis

Physical and neurological exam, ear exam, blood tests, and imaging (MRI or CT) when a brain or middle-ear cause is suspected.

Treatment

Depends on the cause:

  • Old dog vestibular disease: supportive care and anti-nausea medication
  • Ear infections: antibiotics (sometimes long courses)
  • Brain disease: treatment specific to the cause

Caring for your dog at home

  • Clear paths; block stairs and pools; cover slippery floors with rugs
  • Use a harness or towel sling to help with walking
  • Put food and water within easy reach; hand-feed if needed
  • A comfortable bed in a quiet spot
  • Give medications exactly as directed
  • Most dogs adapt quickly, even if a mild head tilt remains

Call us right away if

  • Signs worsen quickly
  • Your dog cannot stand or walk
  • Seizures or collapse
  • Not eating or drinking for more than 24 hours

Outlook

Most dogs with peripheral (inner-ear) disease improve greatly within 2–3 weeks. The outlook for brain-related disease depends on the underlying cause.
