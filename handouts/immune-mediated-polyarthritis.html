Immune-Mediated Polyarthritis (IMPA)

What is polyarthritis?

Poly (many) + arthr (joint) + itis (inflammation) = inflammation in more than one joint. Inflammation is the body's response to injury, infection or an overactive immune system.

Causes

  • Infections (e.g., tick-borne diseases)
  • Reactive — triggered by inflammation elsewhere (heart, uterus, bladder, skin, intestines)
  • Drugs or vaccines; some cancers
  • Idiopathic — no cause found; the immune system attacks the joints (the most common form)

Who is affected?

Usually dogs 3–7 years old (range 6 months–12 years), any breed, males and females equally. Rare in cats.

Signs

  • Stiff, short-strided walking; reluctance to stand or walk
  • Fever, lethargy, poor appetite — often looks like a "sick all over" dog
  • Wrists and ankles most often affected; joints may be swollen, but often are not
  • May occur with inflammation of the brain, muscles, skin, liver or intestines

Diagnosis

  • Physical exam; blood tests, urinalysis (checking for protein loss) and blood pressure
  • Tick-borne and other infectious disease testing
  • X-rays of the chest and joints; sometimes abdominal ultrasound
  • Joint taps — fluid from at least 4 joints is needed to diagnose IMPA. Many inflammatory cells suggest IMPA (or infection).

Treatment

  • Prednisone daily until signs resolve, then gradually reduced
  • A second immune-suppressing drug is often added so steroids can be lowered:
    • Mycophenolate — diarrhea is common; blood problems are rare
    • Leflunomide — mild side effects (poor appetite, tiredness, vomiting); regular blood tests needed
    • Other options are available
  • If signs return, long-term medication may be needed

Follow-up

  • If there is no improvement within 7 days of steroids, the diagnosis is re-evaluated
  • Regular blood and urine tests; rising urine protein may signal kidney damage
  • A blood test (CRP) may help track response; repeat joint taps are not usually needed

Outlook

About 90% respond quickly to treatment. About 10% do not and develop ongoing lameness and joint damage. Long-term steroid side effects are a common concern, which is why we aim to taper to the lowest effective dose.
